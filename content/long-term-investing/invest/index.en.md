## How I Understood Investing at the Start

When I first started looking at investing seriously, what I cared about most was the rate of return.

Bank deposits, government bonds, bonds, the S&P index, small-cap stocks, real estate, venture capital — every asset class seemed to have a rough long-term return range. Back then I was easily drawn in by those numbers, thinking that as long as I found assets with higher returns, the problem would be solved.

Later I gradually realized that returns are only the surface.

What's really hard is: whether you can hold on, whether you can withstand drawdowns, whether you'll be forced to sell when the family needs money, and whether you'll panic when income becomes unstable.

So investing isn't simply about comparing whose returns are higher. For an ordinary family, it's more about putting different assets in the right places.

## Why I Later Moved to Index Funds

Later on I leaned more and more toward index funds, for simple reasons.

First, they're hassle-free.

It's hard for ordinary people to judge over the long run whether an individual stock is cheap, and hard to keep tracking changes in a company's business. Work, family, and cash flow are complicated enough — it's impossible to put all your energy into picking stocks.

Second, they're cheap.

Index funds have low fees, clear rules, and no need for frequent trading. Over the long run, paying less in fees is itself part of the return.

Third, they're diversified enough.

Take the S&P 500: you're not betting on one company, you're buying a basket of core US companies. It won't make you the most extreme money, but it can avoid a lot of single-company risk.

That's also why I later allocated to assets like VOO, VTI, 513500, the Nasdaq, and VGT. None of them is perfect; it's that they can each take on a different role fairly clearly.

## Asset Allocation Matters More Than Picking Instruments

I used to ask: Is this ETF good? Will this sector go up? Is this fund manager any good?

Now I care more about: what does it do in the portfolio?

Some assets are responsible for long-term growth, like VOO, VTI, VGT, and the Nasdaq.

Some assets are responsible for reducing volatility, like short-term bonds, money market funds, and SGOV.

Some assets are responsible for defense and hedging, like gold.

And some assets are just small-position observations, like BTC, ETH, and AI Crypto.

If an asset's place can't be explained clearly, then even if it has risen a lot recently, it may not be suitable for the portfolio.

The point of asset allocation isn't to buy a little of everything evenly; it's to keep the portfolio from being too fragile.

Being able to participate when markets rise, hold up when markets fall, and not be forced to sell long-term assets when something comes up at home — that matters more than beating others in the short term.

## How I See This Old Note Now

This article is a relatively early investing note. Some of the ideas in it still hold up today, like valuing index funds, valuing diversification, and valuing the long term.

But I no longer write about investing as a pile of return figures and principles.

I prefer to look at it from the perspective of a family asset system: salary, mortgage, cash pool, index funds, gold, bonds, and crypto assets — where each one sits, how large its share is, and what to do when something goes wrong.

So this piece can be seen as a starting point.
